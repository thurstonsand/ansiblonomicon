# Lighting primer

## Verdict

Do not replace the old grid of recessed cans with a more expensive grid of recessed cans. Build each room from three separately controlled layers: soft ambient light on walls and ceilings, task light where eyes and hands work, and a small amount of accent light. Use recessed fixtures only where their directional beam solves a specific problem, such as a countertop, shower, passage, artwork, or a high-ceilinged area where another fixture is impractical. Deep regress, shielding, careful aiming, and dimming matter more than a fashionable trim.

For ordinary warm-white interior light, specify one CCT family, usually 2700 K in living and sleeping rooms and 2700 K or 3000 K in work rooms, with CRI at least 90 and good red rendering. Ask for IES TM-30 data rather than treating CRI as the whole answer. Put every layer on its own control and require a full-size dimming mock-up before buying integrated fixtures. A fixture that flickers, pops on at 10 percent, changes color while dimming, or exposes a painfully bright aperture has failed even if its brochure is immaculate.

Yuji and Hue solve different problems. Yuji's fixed-white lamps have materially stronger published color-rendering specifications and belong in reading, food preparation, dressing, art, and other color-sensitive light. Hue's Signe, Play Gradient Tube, Play Bar, and Play Wall Washer are credible accent and wall-lighting tools, but Hue specifies only CRI 80 or better on the products where it publishes CRI. Their gradients can resemble the promotional images on a nearby light-colored wall, but no qualifying independent test located for this research measured the projected effect. Treat them as accent light, mock them up in the actual room, and do not ask them to carry task lighting.

The clean house strategy is Yuji or an equivalent high-fidelity fixed-white source on smart switches and compatible phase-cut dimmers, plus Hue integrated fixtures on constant power for color accents. Do not put a Hue bulb behind a wall dimmer. Scenes can coordinate both systems through the controls without forcing one lamp to do both jobs.

## Fundamentals

### Design layers, not a ceiling grid

The Illuminating Engineering Society's residential recommended practice explicitly treats downlighting, vertical-surface lighting, wall washing, accent lighting, indirect lighting, and cove lighting as distinct tools. That is the useful frame: illumination is a composition, not a fixture count. [IES RP-11-20, table of contents](https://store.ies.org/product/rp-11-20-recommended-practice-lighting-for-interior-and-exterior-residential-environments/)

- **Ambient light** supplies comfortable background visibility and navigation. It can come from shaded pendants, decorative fixtures, sconces, coves, floor and table lamps, and light bounced from walls or ceilings. It need not make a room equally bright everywhere.
- **Task light** puts more light on a defined activity: counters, sink, desk, reading chair, mirror, workbench, closet. Locating it near the task gets useful illuminance without making the entire room bright. DOE's energy-effective checklist likewise notes that local task light can permit lower ambient levels. [DOE/FEMP lighting checklist](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/femp/pdfs/lighting_checklst.pdf)
- **Accent light** creates visual hierarchy by highlighting art, texture, plants, shelving, or architecture. A few deliberate bright areas read as depth. Lighting everything equally erases that hierarchy.

An all-can room tends to put many small, high-luminance sources in the ceiling, cast hard pools downward, leave walls and faces comparatively dim, and produce repeated glare whenever the apertures enter the field of view. It also makes one circuit responsible for ambient, task, and accent work. More lumens do not repair that geometry.

### Indirect and bounced light

A wall or ceiling becomes a large, low-luminance secondary source when a concealed or shielded fixture lights it evenly. That softens shadows, raises vertical brightness, and reduces direct view of the LED. It is often the most effective antidote to the cave-like combination of bright floor and dark walls produced by downlights.

The trade is efficiency and dependence on finishes. Dark, saturated, rough, or very high ceilings absorb or scatter more light; glossy surfaces reveal hot spots; a wall washer too close to the wall produces a bright scallop rather than an even field. Indirect light can still glare if it creates a concentrated ceiling hot spot or exposes the source. Mock up fixture distance, aiming, wall color, and furniture before fixing outlets or blocking.

### Where recessed light is appropriate

Use a recessed downlight when a controlled downward or adjustable beam is the right optical tool:

- over kitchen work surfaces when cabinets or pendants cannot carry the task light;
- in showers and wet locations with the proper wet-location rating;
- for circulation, closets, and utility spaces where visual quiet and head clearance matter;
- as an adjustable accent aimed at art or a feature, not at the middle of an empty floor;
- in unusually high or constrained ceilings where maintainable alternatives do not work.

Prefer fewer, well-placed architectural fixtures over a uniform grid. Ask for deep-regressed emitters, baffles or shielding, beam angle, photometric files, and the actual cutoff angle. Place lights from the furniture and task plan, not from equal ceiling spacing. A downlight over a person's head produces facial shadows; light near the front of a counter or from under a cabinet usually serves the task better.

### CCT and consistency

Correlated color temperature describes the apparent warm-to-cool character of nominally white light, not color-rendering quality. DOE warns that CCT condenses color coordinates with significant information loss. Two products marked 2700 K can still differ in tint, especially across brands and dim levels. [DOE, LED Basics](https://www.energy.gov/eere/ssl/led-basics)

Use 2700 K as the default for relaxed residential rooms. Consider 3000 K for kitchens, baths, workshops, and offices if the finishes and daylight support it. The precise split is preference, but adjacent sources visible together should match. Specify the same nominal CCT and, for architectural products, tight chromaticity tolerance. Review them together at full and low output. If warm-dim is desired, use it consistently within the scene rather than mixing a fixed 2700 K lamp with a downlight that shifts toward amber.

Tunable white is useful where a room changes roles through the day, but controls must make it simple. A technically available spectrum buried in an app is not a lighting plan.

### CRI, R9, and TM-30

DOE recommends CRI 80 as a minimum for interiors and describes CRI 90 or higher as excellent fidelity, but also says CRI is poor at predicting saturated-red fidelity. R9 is the supplemental saturated-red sample. Reds matter to skin, food, wood, textiles, and art, so two lamps with the same Ra can look quite different. [DOE, LED Basics](https://www.energy.gov/eere/ssl/led-basics)

IES TM-30 is the better specification language. It evaluates many more color samples and reports at least:

- **Rf**, average color fidelity, where higher values mean a closer match to the reference;
- **Rg**, average gamut, where values around 100 mean similar average saturation, above 100 more saturation, and below 100 less;
- a color vector graphic and hue-bin values showing where shifts occur rather than hiding them in one average.

IES notes that the TM-30 red-hue fidelity measure is conceptually similar to using R9 beside CRI but better represents human color vision. There is no single universally correct Rg: fidelity, mild saturation, and preference are different design goals. Ask the designer for the complete TM-30 report and spectral power distribution for shortlisted sources, not only an Ra badge. [IES, Using TM-30 to Improve Your Lighting Design](https://ies.org/fires/using-tm-30-to-improve-your-lighting-design); [ANSI/IES TM-30-24](https://store.ies.org/product/technical-memorandum-ies-method-for-evaluating-light-source-color-rendition)

### Glare and UGR

Glare comes from excessive luminance and contrast in the observer's field of view. Small exposed LEDs and shallow downlights are common offenders because a large amount of light leaves a tiny bright area. Control it with larger luminous surfaces, shielding, deep regress, careful sightlines and aiming, lower ambient output, and brighter walls that reduce contrast.

Unified Glare Rating is a calculated discomfort-glare metric for a specified room, luminaire layout, finishes, and observer position. It is useful when a designer can calculate a representative view, especially in an office, but a bare “UGR < 19” product claim does not prove a residential installation will be comfortable. Ask for the actual calculation or photometric model and inspect the critical seated and reclining sightlines. The luminaire and room create glare together. DOE maintains glare metrics and test methods alongside the IES measurement standards. [DOE, Metrics and Test Methods](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/ssl/metrics-and-test-methods)

### Dimming and flicker

“Dimmable” only means that some combination can dim; it does not guarantee the installed dimmer, driver, load, and wiring work together. Before procurement, require the fixture maker's compatibility list and test the exact lamp count and control. Record:

- minimum stable output and whether it is low enough for evening use;
- smoothness, pop-on, drop-out, dead travel, and audible noise;
- color shift at low output;
- behavior after a power failure and with smart-switch neutral requirements;
- temporal light modulation at full and low output.

Flicker can become worse under dimming even when full output appears stable. ENERGY STAR's lamp specification defines instrumented flicker measurements at full and dimmed conditions and references IES LM-79, TM-30, and NEMA temporal-light-artifact methods. DOE's CALiPER testing also found that LED PAR38 dimming behavior depended on the lamp and dimmer combination. In other words, compatibility tables and mock-ups are engineering, not fussiness. [ENERGY STAR Lamps V2.1 specification](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/ENERGY%20STAR%20Lamps%20V2.1%20Final%20Specification.pdf); [DOE CALiPER 20.2, dimming and flicker](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/publications/pdfs/ssl/caliper_20-2_par38.pdf)

### Does the promotional look hold up?

**Plausible, but not proven by independent measurements.** The optics and rated output support a visible wall effect, and the new Wall Washer has three separately controlled modules designed to spread a gradient. The Signe has enough white output to contribute useful bounced ambient light. The compact tube is brighter than three Play Bars on paper; one Play Bar is only a localized glow.

No review located under the ticket's evidence rule published independent measured illuminance maps, color-mode lumen output, spectral/color accuracy, wall distance, wall reflectance, and repeatable methodology for these exact fixtures. Trusted Reviews says it measures output, but its Wall Washer article reports the same 1,035-lumen manufacturer figure and no independent results or setup, so it does not validate the claim. [Trusted Reviews, Play Wall Washer methodology and article](https://www.trustedreviews.com/reviews/philips-hue-play-wall-washer)

The promotional result therefore remains conditional:

- A matte white or pale wall in a dim room will look closest to the images. Dark paint absorbs output and changes the perceived color.
- Distance broadens and blends the beam but lowers illuminance. Too close reveals hot spots and separate color zones.
- Ambient daylight and conventional room light wash out saturated effects.
- Camera exposure and white balance can make promotional gradients appear brighter and more saturated than an eye sees.
- A reflected 1,035 lumens is accent light, not automatically room ambient light. The wall's area and reflectance decide the result.

Buy or borrow one candidate and mock it up after dark at the intended wall. Photograph it only after judging it by eye. Test warm white as seriously as saturated color, because that is how it will earn daily use. The Wall Washer is the best compact trial; the Signe floor is the better candidate where bounced white light must make a meaningful ambient contribution.

## Yuji high-CRI lamps versus ordinary LED and Hue

Yuji's current North American screw-in products are fixed-white lamps, not smart RGB lamps. Representative official specifications include:

- **CRI-MAX A19/E26**, 11 W, 1,000 lm, 2700 K or 3000 K, CRI 95+, 200-degree beam, phase-cut dimmable with a claimed lower limit of 5 percent. [Yuji CRI-MAX A19 product and data sheet links](https://store.yujiintl.com/products/cri-max-cri-95-a19-a60-11w-dimmable-led-bulb-2700k-3000k)
- **SunWave A19/E26**, 11 W, 3000 K, CRI 98, marketed as flicker-free and dimmable. Its page links the 120 V manufacturer data sheet. [Yuji SunWave A19](https://store.yujiintl.com/products/sunwave-cri-98-a19-a60-flicker-free-wellness-lighting-11w-dimmable-led-bulb-3000k)
- **CRI-MAX BR30/E26**, 16 W, 1,200 lm, 3000 K, 180-degree beam, CRI 95+, claimed R9 up to 95+, Rf 92 and Rg 100, but **not dimmable** in this version. [Yuji CRI-MAX BR30](https://store.yujiintl.com/products/cri-max-cri-95-16w-br30-led-bulb-3000k)
- **CRI-MAX adjustable PAR30/E26**, 16 W, 1,044 lm at 3000 K, CRI 95+, adjustable 10–60-degree beam, TRIAC dimmable. [Yuji CRI-MAX PAR30](https://store.yujiintl.com/products/cri-max-cri-95-16w-dimmable-angle-adjustable-par30-led-bulb-3000k)
- **CRI-MAX GU10 in MR16 shape**, 6 W, 400 lm, 2700 K, CRI 95+, 45-degree beam, 120 V TRIAC dimmable. [Yuji CRI-MAX GU10](https://store.yujiintl.com/products/cri-max-cri-95-gu10-6w-dimmable-led-spotlight-2700k)
- Yuji also lists high-CRI filament/decorative lamps, flexible LED strips, photography modules and higher-output specialty lamps. The catalog changes, so verify voltage, base, enclosure rating, dimmability, and availability for each SKU rather than inferring family-wide behavior. [Yuji high-CRI bulb catalog](https://store.yujiintl.com/collections/high-cri-led-bulbs)

Yuji's published comparison shows a representative standard LED at R9 13 and its 3000 K LED at R9 96. That illustrates the red-rendering problem, but it is a manufacturer-selected comparison, not evidence that every ordinary LED has poor R9. Some mainstream 90+ CRI lamps are good. Compare exact data and samples. [Yuji high-CRI technology](https://store.yujiintl.com/collections/high-cri-led-technology)

Yuji says its CRI-MAX family reaches CRI 97 with minimum 95, TLCI 97, average Rf 92, and Rg 100; some individual pages state R9 above 90 or up to 95. Those are meaningfully stronger published targets than Hue's ≥80 CRI. They still do not replace a per-SKU TM-30 report, especially because “up to” and family averages are not guaranteed values for every CCT and form factor. Yuji explains and publishes TM-30 metrics, but request the actual report or IES/SPD file for the exact lamp being specified. [Yuji TM-30 overview](https://www.yujiintl.com/tm-30-18-high-fidelity-full-color-gamut-led-lighting.html)

### Comparison and house strategy

| Attribute       | Ordinary commodity LED                         | Yuji fixed-white                                                     | Hue color/gradient                                                |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main strength   | Low price, broad availability, high efficacy   | High-fidelity white light and strong published red rendering         | Tunable white, saturated color, gradients, scenes and Hue control |
| Color evidence  | Often CRI 80 or 90; R9/TM-30 commonly absent   | CRI 95–98 claims; selected products publish R9 and TM-30 values      | These integrated products publish CRI ≥80 or omit it; no R9/TM-30 |
| White choice    | Many fixed CCT and warm-dim options            | Mostly fixed CCT by SKU                                              | 2000–6500 K tunable white                                         |
| Dimming/control | SKU and dimmer dependent                       | SKU dependent; some TRIAC dimmable, some explicitly not              | Electronic dimming through Hue; requires constant mains power     |
| Best use here   | Utility and low-priority spaces after sampling | Reading, kitchen work, dressing, mirrors, art, warm ambient fixtures | Wall wash, media, decorative color, low-stakes ambient accents    |

Use Yuji in replaceable-bulb fixtures on neutral-equipped smart switches. Where dimming is needed, choose a Yuji SKU explicitly marked dimmable and a dimmer Yuji has tested, then verify the real circuit at minimum load. A smart switch can automate power and scenes without degrading the lamp's white-light spectrum. A smart dimmer adds control but also becomes part of the lamp-driver compatibility problem.

Keep Hue fixtures continuously powered and control them by Hue commands, Hue remotes, or a wall control designed not to cut their power. Coordinate “evening,” “reading,” and “entertaining” scenes in Home Assistant or the chosen control system: fixed-white task fixtures can turn on or dim while Hue accents choose a complementary warm white or color. This makes quality of light and smart color coexist at the room level. Combining them inside one fixture would require separate tested light engines and controls and gains little.

Before standardizing Yuji, compare the bulbs already in hand against a good mainstream 90+ CRI lamp and incandescent or daylight reference using skin, food, wood, art, and saturated red fabric. Also test low-level dimming, camera slow-motion for obvious modulation, audible noise, enclosed-fixture heat, and restart behavior. The eye should approve the spectrum; the dimmer must approve the electronics. Both have veto power.

## Outdoor lighting

Use the joint DarkSky and IES principles: light must be useful, targeted, low-level, controlled, and warm-colored. Fully shield fixtures so no light leaves above horizontal, aim them down onto the path, stair, door, or work area, and avoid shining into windows, trees, neighboring property, or the sky. DarkSky's home guidance recommends 2700 K or lower where possible; its residential certification limit is 3000 K. [DarkSky and IES Five Principles](https://darksky.org/resources/guides-and-how-tos/lighting-principles); [DarkSky home assessment](https://darksky.org/get-involved/home-lighting-assessment/); [DarkSky luminaire criteria](https://darksky.org/what-we-do/darksky-approved/darksky-approved-luminaires-program/darksky-approved-luminaires-guidelines/)

Prefer motion or occupancy activation for bright security and work light, with a short, adjustable timeout and manual override. For arrival and wayfinding, a low dim level from dusk until a reasonable curfew can make the house legible without flooding the site; raise it on motion. Reserve dusk-to-dawn operation for the few lights that have a real all-night safety or identification job. DOE's exterior controls guide distinguishes photocell/astronomical dusk-to-dawn control from scheduled shutoff and occupancy strategies; zoning each use separately avoids the usual all-night compromise. [DOE, Exterior Lighting Control Guidance](https://betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/exterior-lighting-control-guidance.pdf)

Specify 2200–2700 K for routine exterior light, no brighter than the task requires. Use good shielding even at 2700 K. Motion sensing does not excuse a badly aimed floodlight, and a shield does not excuse leaving it at full output until dawn.

## Questions to bring to a lighting designer

1. Can you draw the ambient, task, and accent layers separately for every room, tied to the furniture and art plan rather than a ceiling grid?
2. Which walls and ceilings should carry bounced light, and what output, offset, beam distribution, and finish reflectance does the calculation assume?
3. Which recessed fixtures solve a specific task, and can we reduce their count? What are their cutoff angles, aperture luminance, beam options, and critical seated sightlines?
4. What illuminance and vertical-luminance targets will you use for counters, reading chairs, mirrors, desks, stairs, art, and circulation? Show the calculation and assumptions.
5. What CCT strategy connects adjacent rooms by day and night? Where, if anywhere, is warm-dim or tunable white worth its control complexity?
6. For every primary white source, can you provide CRI, R9, the complete current IES TM-30 report, SPD, chromaticity tolerance, and color consistency over dimming and product life?
7. What glare analysis applies to the actual layout? If UGR is quoted, what room geometry, finishes, observer position, and photometric file produced it?
8. Which exact driver, dimmer, smart switch, and load combinations does the manufacturer support? What are measured minimum output, pop-on, dropout, noise, and flicker at full and low levels?
9. Can we build one representative full-size mock-up of a recessed task light, an indirect wall or cove, a Yuji reading fixture, and a Hue wall washer before fixture schedules are locked?
10. How will each layer remain manually usable if Home Assistant, Hue Bridge, the LAN, or the internet is unavailable?
11. Which integrated fixtures have replaceable drivers or light engines, and what is the replacement path when an integrated Hue or architectural LED fails?
12. Outdoors, which locations genuinely need all-night low light, which should use motion, and how will shielding, 2200–2700 K sources, curfew schedules, and property-line spill be verified after dark?
